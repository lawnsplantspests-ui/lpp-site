What a Lead-Gen Site Actually Is

A lead-generation site looks like a local company. It has a town name in the title, pictures of wasp nests, and a big "call now" button. But nobody behind it removes anything. When you call, the call gets routed to a network of contractors, and the site gets paid a fee for sending it.

That doesn't automatically mean you'll get a bad job. It means you don't know who's coming, whether they're licensed, or what they'll charge until someone is already in your driveway.

The Fine Print Usually Gives It Away

Scroll to the very bottom of the page. Lead-gen sites almost always carry a disclaimer, and the wording is pretty consistent. Watch for lines saying:

  • calls are routed to an "advertising partner"
  • the site "may be paid a referral fee"
  • the site doesn't provide the service itself and isn't affiliated with the companies it connects you to
  • it doesn't guarantee the work those companies do

If you see that, you're not looking at a pest control company. You're looking at an ad.

Six Quick Checks Before You Call

  • Is there a real street address? Not "serving Middletown" or "Harrisburg area," but an actual address you could drive to.
  • Is the number local? An 800, 877, or 888 number isn't proof by itself, but most local operators around here answer a 717 number.
  • Does every town get the same page? If the site has a page for dozens of towns with the same paragraphs and only the town name swapped, it's usually a template built to catch searches.
  • Is a real person named? A local company can tell you who owns it. A lead-gen site has stock photos and no names.
  • Can they tell you who's coming? Ask on the phone. If they can't say who will show up, the call has been passed along.
  • For wasps, yellow jackets, and hornets, ask for the license number. Pennsylvania requires any business that applies pesticides on someone else's property for hire to hold a commercial pesticide license, with a certified applicator on staff. A legitimate company gives you the number without hesitating. You can confirm it with the Pennsylvania Department of Agriculture.
Honey bees are a different check. Live honey bee removal is beekeeping work. No pesticide is involved, so a pesticide license isn't the right question. Ask whether they remove the colony alive, and where the bees go afterward. A colony that gets sprayed inside a wall dies there, and the leftover comb and honey can leak and attract other pests. Why This Matters Most With Stinging Insects

Nobody shops around for a yellow jacket nest next to the back door. You call the first number you find. That's exactly the kind of call lead-gen sites are built for. It's also when people end up with someone who isn't licensed, or who turns a one-time nest into a yearly contract pitch.

Taking two minutes to check the fine print and ask for a license number is worth it, even in a hurry.
