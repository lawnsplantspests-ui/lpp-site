What They Actually Are

Not a spray, and not a chemical. Beneficial nematodes are microscopic roundworms that occur naturally in soil, raised commercially and applied live. They move through soil moisture hunting insect larvae. When one finds a grub it enters, releases the bacteria it carries, and the larva dies within a couple of days. The nematodes then reproduce inside it and the next generation goes hunting.

That's the whole mechanism. It's predation, not poisoning — which is why they're so narrow in what they affect.

What They Control

Different nematode species hunt differently — some cruise through soil after deep targets, others wait near the surface for things moving past. Matching the species to the pest is most of the job.

White grubs

Japanese beetle, masked chafer, and June beetle larvae — the ones that sever roots and bring skunks and raccoons digging. See something tore my lawn up overnight.

Root weevils

Black vine weevil especially — the larvae that chew roots on rhododendron, azalea, yew, and hosta while the adults notch the leaf edges above. One of the best uses for nematodes anywhere.

Borer larvae

Peachtree borer and dogwood borer, where larvae are working in the lower trunk and crown zone rather than high in the canopy.

Turf caterpillars

Sod webworm and cutworms, which feed at the soil surface and thatch line.

Fleas and ticks

The larval and nymph stages living in shaded ground, leaf litter, and woodline edges — see mosquito & tick control.

Fungus gnats

Larvae in the soil of outdoor containers and beds.

What they don't touch People, pets, birds, fish, earthworms, bees, and every other pollinator. They don't harm the plant, they leave no residue, and there's no re-entry interval — nobody has to stay off the lawn afterward. Their safety profile is why they're exempt from EPA pesticide registration.

The Honest Trade-Offs

We'd rather tell you this up front than have you disappointed in September.

  • Usually not as complete as a synthetic preventive. A well-timed conventional grub treatment generally gives higher and more predictable knockdown. Nematodes work — they just work like biology rather than chemistry.
  • Conditions have to be right. They need soil moisture and a soil temperature in range. Cold soil, bone-dry soil, or ground baked hard in August will waste the application. This is the single biggest reason people try them once, see nothing, and conclude they don't work.
  • They're alive, which means handling matters. They have a shelf life, they can't cook in a hot truck, and they need to be watered into the soil rather than left on the surface. A bag bought at a big-box store in July and left in a car has usually already failed before it goes down.
  • Judge them over a season, not a week. There's no dramatic overnight kill to look at.
  • They won't fix a lawn that's dying of something else. If the brown patch is drought or disease, nematodes do nothing. We check that before quoting — see is it actually grubs?

Getting the Timing Right

Timing is most of whether this works. Nematodes have to go in when the target larvae are actively feeding and reachable, and when soil conditions will keep them alive long enough to hunt.

For white grubs in Central PA that generally means late summer into early autumn, while grubs are feeding near the surface and soil is still warm. Note this is the biological alternative to the conventional path — a systemic preventive in May or June, or a curative product in September or October if turf is already being destroyed. Root weevil and borer timing runs on its own schedule. Soil gets watered before and after so they move down out of the sun and into the root zone rather than sitting on top of the thatch.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are these the same nematodes that damage plants?
No, and it's a fair question because the word covers both. Plant-parasitic nematodes attack roots and are a problem. Beneficial or entomopathogenic nematodes attack insect larvae and don't feed on plants at all. Different animals, opposite effect.
Can I just buy them myself?
You can, and for a small area it's a reasonable thing to try. The two things that go wrong are handling and timing — they're a live product with a shelf life that dies in heat and sunlight, and an application made at the wrong soil temperature accomplishes nothing. If you want to try it yourself we'll tell you what to look for rather than pretend it's impossible.
Do they stay in my soil permanently?
Some persist and reproduce for a while where conditions suit them, but don't count on a one-time application solving it forever. Treat them as a seasonal control, not a permanent colony.
Is this safe if I keep bees?
Yes — this is exactly why we offer it. Nematodes stay in the soil and have no effect on bees at any stage. We keep our own hives and this is the option we reach for on properties where pollinator protection is the priority. See pollinator restoration.
How much does a nematode treatment cost?
It's priced on the quantity of nematodes released and how many plants or how much ground that has to cover — a few shrubs is a small release, a full lawn is a much larger one. There's no flat rate we could quote you honestly without knowing the scope. Tell us what you're treating and roughly how much of it and we'll size it and price it. Estimates are free.
Will it hurt my earthworms?
No. Beneficial nematodes target insect larvae specifically. Earthworms aren't insects and aren't affected, which matters because a healthy worm population is doing real work in your soil.
