Is It Carpenter Ants, Termites, or Just Ants?

This ID matters more than most, because the three call for completely different responses — and one of them is a much bigger financial problem than the others.

Carpenter Ants

Large (roughly ¼ to ½ inch), usually black, with a pinched waist, bent (elbowed) antennae, and one uneven pair of wings if swarming. They excavate wood to nest in it — they don't eat it. Galleries look smooth and sanded, and they push debris out.

Termites

Straight-sided body with no pinched waist, straight antennae, and two equal pairs of wings. They eat wood, leave mud in their galleries, and build mud tubes on foundations. Faster, costlier damage — and a different treatment entirely. If that's what you have, we'll say so.

Nuisance Ants

Odorous house ants and pavement ants are small, trail in numbers along counters and walkways, and cause no structural damage. Annoying, and a much simpler job — see general pest control.

The Sawdust Clue

Carpenter ants push out "frass" — fine wood shavings mixed with insect parts and dead ants, usually piling below a crack, window frame, or door casing. That pile means a nest above it, not just a passing forager. It's the single most useful thing you can photograph for us.

They're Pointing at a Water Problem

Carpenter ants strongly prefer wood that is or has been damp and softened. Find the water and you've usually found the nest — which is why a treatment that ignores the moisture is a treatment you'll be buying again.

Usual Suspects

Leaking or undersized gutters, roof and flashing leaks, window and door frames with failed caulk, the wall behind a shower or dishwasher, sill plates in damp crawl spaces, and porch columns and deck ledgers.

Parent and Satellite Nests

The main colony is often outdoors — a stump, a dead limb, a woodpile, a hollow tree — with satellite nests indoors. That's why killing the ants you see indoors changes nothing: the queen is somewhere else, resupplying. Treatment has to reach the whole system.

Spring Swarms

Winged carpenter ants appearing indoors in spring is a strong sign of an established indoor nest, not a wandering scout. Ants outdoors are normal; a swarm at your window in March is a phone call.

Carpenter Ant FAQ

I sprayed the ants I saw and now there are more. Why?
Most over-the-counter sprays are repellent — they kill the ants they contact and push the rest to relocate, which frequently splits a colony into additional satellite nests inside your walls. It's the classic way a small problem becomes a distributed one.
How fast do they damage a house?
Much slower than termites — this is years, not months, and a mature colony is required before damage becomes structural. That's the good news. The bad news is that the moisture that invited them is usually doing its own damage on a parallel track, so "slow" is not a reason to wait.
Do I need to tear open the wall to find the nest?
Usually not. Trail patterns, frass locations, sounding the wood, and where activity peaks at dusk narrow it down well enough to treat effectively. Demolition is a last resort, not a starting point.
Will you spray my whole house inside?
No — we never spray inside. Our pest control is perimeter-only across the board: exterior foundation, eaves, soffits, and entry points, so pests are stopped at the door instead of treated in your kitchen. If a situation genuinely requires interior application, we'll tell you honestly and point you to someone who does that work.
These are in my deck rails with round holes — same thing?
That sounds like carpenter bees, which are a completely different animal: single large bees drilling perfectly round holes into fascia, rails, and cedar trim. See our carpenter bee guide.
