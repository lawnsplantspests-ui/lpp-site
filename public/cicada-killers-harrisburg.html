The Two Things You Want to Know Right Now

The Aggressive Ones Can't Hurt You

Male cicada killers are territorial and theatrical — they hover, dart at faces, and chase each other and you around the yard. They also physically cannot sting. No stinger. It's entirely a bluff, and it's the reason these wasps have a far worse reputation than they deserve.

The Females Can, and Almost Never Do

Females do have a stinger — they use it to paralyze cicadas. But they're solitary, they have no colony to defend, and they're famously reluctant to sting people. Realistically it takes grabbing one barehanded or stepping on one barefoot. Most Central PA homeowners live through an entire cicada killer season without a single sting.

They're Not a Colony

This is the big difference from yellow jackets. Every hole is one female doing her own thing. Twenty holes means twenty independent wasps, not a nest of thousands with a shared alarm signal. Nothing pours out of the ground and comes after you.

They Have a Short Season

Adults show up when annual cicadas do — roughly July into August here — and they're gone by early fall on their own. If you're reading this in late August, the problem is largely solving itself.

Cicada Killer or Yellow Jacket? This Is the One That Matters

Both dig in the ground. One is harmless and one puts people in the emergency room, so getting this right is worth thirty seconds. It's easier than it sounds — look at the holes, not the wasp.

Cicada Killers: Many Holes, One Wasp Each

Lots of separate burrows scattered across an area, each about the width of a finger, each with a small horseshoe-shaped fan of loose soil beside it. One big wasp per hole, coming and going calmly. Often you'll see one dragging a paralyzed cicada — that's conclusive.

Yellow Jackets: One Hole, Constant Traffic

A single entrance with a steady stream of smaller wasps going in and out like an airport. No excavated soil fan. Get within a few feet with a mower and they come out in numbers. That's a real problem — see ground nests and yellow jacket removal.

Size Is a Clue Too

Cicada killers are enormous — an inch and a half to two inches, with rusty-red legs and a black abdomen marked with pale yellow. Yellow jackets are about half an inch and brightly banded. If your reaction was "I've never seen a wasp that big," it's almost certainly a cicada killer.

Not Murder Hornets

Worth saying plainly: the northern giant hornet ("murder hornet") is not established in Pennsylvania. Every giant-wasp panic call we get here turns out to be a cicada killer or a European hornet. Send a photo and we'll settle it in a minute.

Why They Picked Your Yard

Cicada killers are picky about real estate, and what they want tells you how to get rid of them long-term. They dig in soil that is bare or thinly covered, well-drained, and in full sun. Thick, healthy turf is close to useless to them.

Where They Show Up

Thin or bare patches in the lawn, sandy or sandy-loam soil, the strip along a south-facing sidewalk or driveway, bare ground under a swing set, sparse areas beneath big shade trees, slopes and berms, and the edges of ball fields and sand traps.

What That Means

A cicada killer problem is usually a turf problem wearing a costume. They found the one part of your yard where grass isn't thriving. Kill this year's wasps and next July they'll be back, because the bare spot is still there advertising.

The Long-Term Fix

Thicken the turf in the areas they're using — overseeding, correcting the soil, and consistent watering through their nesting window all make that ground far less attractive. It's slower than a spray and it's the only thing that actually ends the cycle. See lawn care.

They're Doing Something Useful

Each female hunts cicadas, paralyzes them, and hauls them underground to feed one larva. They're also decent pollinators. As a beekeeping family we're not in a hurry to kill a native solitary wasp that isn't hurting anybody — and we'll say so.

Usually Leave Them

A handful of burrows out in the open lawn, in a corner of the yard, along a fence line, or anywhere people aren't walking barefoot. They'll be gone by September. Fill the holes after they finish and get grass growing there.

Worth Treating

Heavy activity in genuinely high-traffic areas — around a pool, a play set, a front walk, a mailbox, a business entrance. Also when someone in the household has a sting allergy, or when the sheer number of burrows is causing real turf and soil erosion.

Cicada Killer FAQ

One keeps flying right at my face. Is it going to sting me?
Almost certainly not — that behavior is male territorial patrolling, and males have no stinger. He's checking whether you're a rival male or a female. Walk through; he'll break off. It is genuinely unnerving the first few times, and it is genuinely harmless.
Are they dangerous to my kids or dog?
Very little risk. No colony means no mass defensive response, which is what makes yellow jackets dangerous. The realistic scenario is a barefoot kid stepping directly on a female. If the burrows are right where kids play, that's exactly the situation where treatment makes sense — and where we'd recommend it.
Can I just fill in the holes?
She'll dig back out or dig a new one — it's loose soil to her. Filling holes is worth doing in fall after the season ends, paired with seed, so the spot isn't bare ground again next July.
Will they damage my lawn?
A few burrows, no. A heavy colony-scale aggregation of dozens in one area can genuinely loosen soil, kill patches, and cause erosion on a slope — and that's a legitimate reason to act. Send a photo of the area and we'll tell you which situation you have.
Same thing as ground bees or ground hornets?
No — "ground bees" locally almost always means yellow jackets, which are a completely different and genuinely aggressive problem. See our ground bee and ground nest page. The tell is one busy hole (yellow jackets) versus many quiet ones (cicada killers).
How long until they're gone?
Adults are active for a few weeks, roughly July into August in Central PA, tracking the annual cicadas they hunt. By early fall they're done on their own. Next year's generation emerges from those same burrows — which is why fixing the bare ground matters more than killing this year's adults.
