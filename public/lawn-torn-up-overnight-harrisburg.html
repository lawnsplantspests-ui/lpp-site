First: The Tug Test. It Takes Ten Seconds.

Go to the edge of a damaged patch, take a handful of grass, and pull straight up. If it lifts away freely, like pulling up a rug, with no root resistance at all — that's grubs. They've eaten the roots off underneath, which is exactly why an animal was able to peel it back.

Then fold back a one-foot square and count what's in the top few inches of soil. You're looking for fat white C-shaped larvae with brown heads. A few is normal in any Central PA lawn. Around ten or more per square foot is where damage shows and treatment is worth doing.

Who's Doing the Digging

The pattern tells you which animal, which occasionally matters — mostly it tells you how urgently to look for a den.

Skunk — cone-shaped divots

Small, neat, conical holes an inch or two across, scattered across the turf. A skunk noses out grubs individually. If you're also getting a musky smell near the deck or shed, see skunk under your deck.

Raccoon — peeled flaps

Larger sections of sod rolled or flipped back, sometimes in long strips. Raccoons have hands and they use them. Often paired with attic noise. See raccoon in your attic.

Birds — scattered pecking

Starlings and crows working a lawn for grubs leave a fine scatter of small holes rather than excavation. Usually the mildest version, and a useful early warning that grub numbers are climbing.

Moles — raised tunnels

Ridged runways pushed up through the turf rather than holes dug down into it. We don't treat moles — worth saying plainly so you're not paying us to look at something we don't handle.

Make Sure It's Actually Grubs

Brown patches in a Central PA lawn have several possible causes, and grub treatment fixes exactly one of them. Before anyone treats anything:

  • Turf lifts freely with no roots → grubs. This is the reliable one.
  • Brown but firmly rooted, and it's July → almost certainly drought dormancy. Cool-season grass goes dormant and comes back. Don't spend money on it. There's a longer read on telling dead from dormant.
  • Circular patches with distinct edges, often after humid nights → fungal disease, which is a completely different treatment.
  • Small dark-green-ringed dead spots → dog urine.
  • Thinning with lots of crabgrass → a weed and fertility problem, not an insect one.

We check this before quoting. Treating a dormant lawn for grubs is a waste of your money, and we'd rather tell you it's going to rain than sell you an application.

Timing Is Most of the Battle

This is the part that decides whether treatment works. Grubs are the larvae of Japanese beetles, masked chafers, and related beetles. They hatch in mid-to-late summer and feed hard on roots through late summer into autumn, which is when damage and digging appear. They go deep for winter, feed again briefly in spring, then pupate.

  • Preventive applications go down before or around egg hatch — late spring into early summer. Far and away the most effective and the least material. If you had grubs last year, this is what you want this year.
  • Curative applications target grubs already feeding, in late summer and early fall. Effective on small grubs; much less so once they're large and deep.
  • Spring treatment for last fall's damage is mostly too late — those grubs are nearly done feeding. Spring is for repairing the lawn and planning prevention, not for killing.

Repairing the Damage

Where the turf has been peeled but the soil is intact, a lot of it can simply be pressed back down and watered — if you get to it quickly, the roots that are left will often take hold again. Where it's been shredded or the roots are gone entirely, it needs seed. In Central PA the right window for that is early September, when soil temperature and moisture give cool-season seed its best chance before winter. We schedule core aeration and overseeding only in that window, and it books out by Labor Day most years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will the animals come back tonight?
Yes, while the grubs are still there. That's the frustrating part of this problem — it continues nightly until the food runs out. Treatment doesn't stop it instantly either; the digging tapers off over the following week or two as the grub population drops.
I put down a grub product from the hardware store and nothing happened.
Usually a timing problem rather than a product problem. Most retail grub products are preventives, and a preventive applied in September, onto grubs that are already large, does very little. The other common issue is not watering it in — nearly all of these need to move down into the root zone to reach anything.
Do I have to treat the whole lawn?
Not always. Grubs are frequently concentrated in sunnier, more irrigated sections rather than spread evenly. We sample in several places before quoting, and if it's confined to one area we'll treat that area. Blanket-treating an entire property that doesn't need it is neither cheap nor something we're keen on doing.
Is grub treatment safe around pollinators?
It depends entirely on the product and the timing, which is exactly why it matters who's applying it. We keep our own hives, so we choose materials and timing to avoid bloom and avoid flowering weeds in the turf. If you have a pollinator garden, fruit trees, or your own bees, tell us — it changes what we use. See our pollinator-friendly programs.
