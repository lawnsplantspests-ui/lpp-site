Why Fall Is Invasion Season

Every September and October the calls spike, and it isn't coincidence. Field mice that spent summer outdoors start looking for a warm, dry place with food as nights get cold — and a house radiating heat with a gap under the garage door is exactly the advertisement they're reading. Once inside, they don't leave, and they don't wait to reproduce: a female can produce several litters a year, so a couple of mice in October is a population by February.

A Mouse Needs a Dime-Sized Gap

If a pencil fits, a house mouse fits — their skull is the limiting factor and it compresses more than seems possible. Rats need about an inch. That's why "I don't see any holes" almost never means there aren't any.

Where They Actually Enter

Garage door corners with worn weatherstripping, dryer and utility line penetrations, gaps behind siding at the sill plate, foundation cracks, unsealed crawl space vents, and the gap under a bulkhead door. Attached garages are the single most common entry route we find in Harrisburg homes.

The Damage

Contaminated food and surfaces, shredded insulation and stored belongings, and chewed wiring — mice gnaw constantly, and chewed romex in a wall or vehicle is a genuine fire risk. Mice have also caused a lot of very expensive damage to cars parked in barns and garages.

Health Considerations

Droppings and urine can carry pathogens, and dust from an infested space shouldn't be swept up dry. There's a right way to clean a mouse-contaminated area, and we'll walk you through it rather than leave you guessing.

Our Approach: Seal the Building First

Perpetual baiting is a subscription. Exclusion is a solution. We'd rather do the work that ends the problem, even though it means fewer return visits.

1. Find the Openings

A full interior and exterior inspection: sill plates, utility penetrations, garage thresholds, crawl space, and the roofline. Droppings and rub marks tell us the travel routes, which tell us where they're coming in.

2. Seal Them Properly

Copper mesh, hardware cloth, sheet metal, and mortar where appropriate — materials mice can't chew. Expanding foam alone is not a rodent seal, no matter what the can says; they go straight through it.

3. Knock Down the Population Inside

Trapping placed on actual runways rather than scattered hopefully, so the interior population comes down fast and you're not living with dead-mouse odor in a wall.

4. Manage the Exterior

Tamper-resistant, secured stations outside where warranted, placed to intercept rodents before they reach the structure — and placed with pets, kids, and non-target wildlife specifically in mind. As beekeepers and a wildlife operator, we care about what else eats a poisoned rodent, and we'll tell you honestly when baiting isn't the right tool.

5. Remove the Attractions

Pet food and birdseed in sealed bins rather than bags, firewood off the foundation, tall grass and ground cover trimmed back from the wall, and bird feeders relocated. Small changes that decide whether next fall repeats.

Rodent FAQ

I catch one every few days. Is that normal?
It means there's an open door and a supply outside, not that you're winning. Steady catch numbers with no end point is the classic signature of an unsealed structure — which is why we start with the building rather than the traps.
Will poison make a mouse die in my wall?
It can, and it's a real drawback of bait-only programs — there's no way to steer where a rodent dies. That's a significant part of why we favor exclusion plus interior trapping, where we control the outcome and can remove what we catch.
I have pets and a hawk that hunts my field. Is rodenticide safe?
Fair concern, and one we take seriously. Secondary poisoning of raptors, foxes, and cats is a documented problem with certain products. When we do use bait it's in secured, tamper-resistant stations, chosen and placed to minimize that risk — and on plenty of properties we skip it entirely and solve the job with exclusion and trapping.
Mice or rats — does it matter?
Yes. Rats need bigger openings, travel farther, are warier of new objects (which changes trap strategy), and often tie back to burrows outside or a nearby food source. Droppings tell us quickly — mouse droppings are rice-sized, rat droppings are closer to a raisin.
Something's in the wall but it's only at night — mice?
Possibly, but nighttime-only noise can also be rats, flying squirrels, or bats, and daytime noise usually means gray squirrels. Getting the ID right first saves you paying for the wrong solution.
