First: The Timing Is the Whole Thing.

Stink bugs don't breed in your house. They come in from outside, all at once, over a few warm days in autumn, looking for somewhere sheltered to spend the winter. Then they sit dormant in wall voids, attics, and around window frames until warmth wakes them up — which is why you get a fresh wave every sunny day in February, and why people think they're multiplying in there. They aren't. They're the same ones, waking up in shifts.

The window that matters is late September into early October in Central PA — as they start massing on warm walls, before the first real cold snap drives them through the gaps. An exterior application then intercepts them on the siding. The same application in December, once they're behind your drywall, mostly doesn't reach them.

Pennsylvania Didn't Get Unlucky — It Got Them First

Worth knowing, because it explains why this is worse here than most places. The brown marmorated stink bug arrived from Asia and the first established U.S. population was identified in the Lehigh Valley in the late 1990s. Everything since has spread outward from eastern Pennsylvania. We've had them longer than nearly anywhere in the country, and the populations in the Susquehanna Valley reflect that. If it feels like your house gets more than it should, it probably does.

The Four That Do This

They arrive at the same time of year, for the same reason, and the treatment approach is the same for all of them — but it's useful to know which you have.

Brown marmorated stink bug

Shield-shaped, mottled brown, about the size of a fingernail, with pale banding on the antennae and the edge of the abdomen. Clumsy fliers that buzz into lampshades. The smell is the defence — release it when threatened or crushed.

Boxelder bug

Slim, black, with bright red-orange lines on the back. Gathers in large numbers on warm walls, often near boxelder or maple trees. Harmless, but they arrive in real numbers and they stain surfaces when squashed.

Asian lady beetle

Looks like a ladybug but varies from orange to deep red, usually with an M-shaped mark behind the head. These do occasionally bite, and they leave a yellow staining fluid on walls and curtains.

Cluster fly

Larger and more sluggish than a house fly, with golden hairs on the thorax. Clusters in attics and around upper windows. Turns up in numbers on warm winter days for the same reason as the others.

None of these will hurt your house They don't bite (with the occasional exception of lady beetles), they don't breed indoors, they don't eat structural wood, and they don't infest food. This is a nuisance problem, not a damage problem. It's just a nuisance problem at a scale that makes people want to move.

The Part Treatment Can't Do

Exclusion. It's unglamorous and it's permanent, and on a house that gets overwhelmed every year it's worth as much as the spraying:

  • Caulk around window and door frames, and where siding meets trim. These insects get through gaps far smaller than you'd credit.
  • Repair or replace torn screens, including attic and gable vent screening — a favourite route in.
  • Fit door sweeps, especially on the garage service door and the basement bulkhead.
  • Seal utility penetrations where cable, dryer vent, AC line set, and hose bibs come through the wall.
  • Check the chimney chase and soffit returns, which are wide open on a lot of Harrisburg-area houses.

Please Don't

  • Squash them. That's what the smell is for, and boxelder bugs and lady beetles stain.
  • Use the good vacuum. The odour gets into the bag and the machine, and then follows you around the house. Keep a cheap dedicated one, or use the old trick of a knee-high stocking rubber-banded into the wand as a disposable catch bag.
  • Bomb the inside. Foggers don't reach into wall voids where the insects actually are, and a large kill inside a wall leaves a food source that draws carpet and larder beetles — trading a harmless problem for a worse one.
  • Bother with the ultrasonic plug-ins. They don't work on these. They don't work on much.
